Kuskuam Church

Built in the 18th century by Empress Mentewab, Kuskuam Church was once both a church and a royal residence. Centuries later, the site is still alive — not only through its old walls and artifacts, but through the people who continue to care for it every day.

The priest who serves at Kuskuam also manages the small museum inside the compound. He welcomes visitors warmly, walking them through the history of the church and sharing stories that connect the past to the present. Surrounded by ancient manuscripts, crosses, paintings, and ceremonial objects, he spends his days protecting pieces of history that have survived for generations.

What makes Kuskuam feel special is not just its age, but the quiet sense of continuity within it. Prayers still echo through the church, visitors still gather to learn its story, and the work of preserving its history continues through the hands of those who remain devoted to the place. In many ways, Kuskuam is more than a historical site — it is a living space where faith, memory, and everyday life still exist side by side.

Yekolo Temariwoch

Traditional Ethiopian Orthodox Church students who dedicate their lives to learning scripture, chant, and cultural teachings while traveling between churches and monasteries. Their path is not fixed in one place; it is a continuous journey built on faith, patience, and the desire to understand something greater than themselves.

Along the way, they depend on the kindness of the communities they pass through. People offer them meals, support, and small acts of generosity, creating a quiet exchange that has existed for generations. In these moments, giving is not just material — it is part of a shared cultural and spiritual connection that links strangers through respect and tradition.

Beyond their religious studies, these students also carry something deeper: the preservation of history, language, and cultural identity. Through what they learn and pass on, an unbroken story continues to move from one generation to the next, keeping traditions alive in both memory and practice.

Victory Day Commemoration

This work documents Ethiopian patriots during the commemoration of the historic Battle of Adwa in Gondar. It captures a moment of remembrance where history, identity, and national pride come together in public space.

The people in these photographs are deeply connected to their country. Their presence reflects a lifelong sense of duty shaped by love for their land, culture, and history. For them, it is not seen as an obligation, but as a responsibility rooted in protecting their people, preserving cultural heritage, and honoring the legacy of those who came before them.

The celebration becomes more than an event — it is a living expression of identity. Through gathering and participation, they show pride in a history that remains distinct and unbroken, and a desire to preserve their traditions and values for future generations.

Uncertain Times

I made this work in Gondar during a time when things felt uncertain, and that uncertainty quietly shaped everyday life. People still went about their routines, but there was a heaviness in the air you could feel it in public spaces, in the way people looked around, and in how moments unfolded without much ease.

I documented the presence of the Ethiopian National Defense Force and the Ethiopian Federal Police, but what stayed with me wasn’t just what I saw in formation or movement. It was everything happening around it — the stillness, the distance in people’s eyes, and the sense that everyone was aware of something larger than the moment itself.

What I kept thinking about was how easy it is to see uniforms and forget the people inside them. Each person carries something personal — duty, pressure, fear, pride, hope — and often all at once. Many of them looked young, still growing into roles that ask a lot from them.

This work became less about “events” and more about atmosphere. About how a city holds tension and normal life at the same time. How people continue, adapt, and stay present even when things don’t feel fully stable.

I tried to keep my approach simple and honest — just observing, waiting, and letting moments happen as they were. No staging, no interruption, just real fragments of life as I found them.

In the end, it’s about people. Not power, not performance but people trying to live through a time that doesn’t feel entirely settled.

Beza and Mikiyas

I spent time with two children, Beza and her brother Mikiyas, as they played together in an open field. It was a simple moment, but it stayed with me — the kind of scene where everything feels light, honest, and unforced.

When I asked to take their photos, they said yes right away. No hesitation, just curiosity and a bit of excitement. Beza had this natural ease in front of the camera, not posed or aware in a formal way, but simply present — like she wasn’t trying to be anything other than herself.

What I remember most is how free they felt. Running, laughing, moving without thinking too much about anything beyond the moment. It reminded me how rare that kind of innocence is, and how powerful it can be in its simplicity.

I didn’t direct much. I just watched and let the moment happen as it was.

Yordanos Molla

This project comes from observing my university dormmate, Yordanos Molla. He is a geography student, but also serves as a deacon at church. What really stayed with me is his daily routine. Most mornings, before anyone else is awake, he is already up praying and reading the Bible. After that, he goes to class, focused on his studies. In the evening, he serves at church again, and later he returns to the library to continue his academic work.

Watching him over time made me think differently about discipline and balance. I started to wonder how someone can hold both faith and education so strongly in their everyday life, without letting one cancel the other.

With this work, I simply wanted to follow that idea. To show a life where faith and learning exist together, not in conflict, but as part of the same commitment. For me, it became about dedication, routine, and the strength it takes to keep going every day.

Timket in Debre Markos

I was in Debre Markos during Timket, and the city felt different that day calm, full, and deeply alive at the same time. People gathered slowly, with purpose, filling the streets in a way that felt more like presence than noise.

What I remember most is how connected everything felt. People standing close together, sharing the same space and moment, each carrying their own thoughts but still moving as one.

I didn’t try to shape what I saw. I just stayed with it and documented it as it naturally unfolded.

Everyday Life

In My Way

Some moments don’t ask to be photographed, they just happen quietly in front of you. These photos are pieces of everyday life — people chasing work, resting for a second, laughing with friends, carrying worries nobody sees, and still moving forward.

I’m not trying to show perfection here. Just real life as it feels. The streets, the emotions, the small details we usually walk past without noticing.

Every face has a story. Every corner holds a moment.
